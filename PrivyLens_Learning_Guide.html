Lesson 1

What machine learning actually is

Normal programming: you write the rules. Machine learning: you show examples and the computer finds the rules.

Why rules fail for us

Try writing a rule for "this word is a person's name". "Rahul" is a name, "Rahul" in "Rahul Dravid Road" is a street, "Apple" is a company or a fruit. You can't list every name, and context changes meaning. But you can easily collect thousands of sentences where humans have marked the names. That is a dataset, and ML learns the pattern from it.

That is exactly what the regex baseline showed: for PERSON it scored 0.00, because no pattern describes "a person".

The three ingredients

IngredientMeaningIn PrivyLens
DataExamples with the right answers (labels)~40,000 sentences where every private span is marked
ModelA function with adjustable numbers insideThe Transformer tagger, 1.56 million adjustable numbers
TrainingAutomatically adjusting those numbers until answers are mostly righttrain_tagger.py on Colab

Supervised learning, in one picture

 sentence ──► MODEL (numbers inside) ──► guess: "O O O B-PERSON ..."
                   ▲                               │
                   │                  compare with the human answer
          nudge the numbers  ◄──── "how wrong?" = the LOSS
              (repeat 1,000,000 times)

Our task is called supervised because a "supervisor" (the dataset labels) tells the model the right answer. The specific task, "give every word a label", is sequence labelling, also called token classification or NER (named entity recognition).

Lesson 2

Neural networks: the adjustable machine

A neuron is a weighted vote

A neuron takes numbers in, multiplies each by a weight (how much it matters), adds them up, and passes the result through a small bend called an activation function (ours is GELU). That is all.

  inputs  x1 x2 x3        output = activation( w1·x1 + w2·x2 + w3·x3 + b )
  weights w1 w2 w3                              ▲ the weights are what training changes

A layer is many neurons side by side. In PyTorch that is nn.Linear(128, 256): it turns a list of 128 numbers into a list of 256 numbers using 128×256 weights. A network is layers stacked.

Parameters = all the weights

Your tagger has 1,556,887 parameters. At the start they are random, so the model outputs nonsense. Training is just finding good values for all 1.5 million numbers.

How the numbers get adjusted

  1. Forward pass: feed a sentence in, get a guess out.
  2. Loss: one number saying how wrong the guess was. We use cross-entropy, which punishes confident wrong answers the hardest.
  3. Backward pass (backpropagation): the computer works out, for every weight, "if I increased you slightly, would the loss go up or down?" This is called the gradient. PyTorch does it with loss.backward().
  4. Update: move every weight a tiny step in the direction that lowers the loss. opt.step(). The step size is the learning rate (5e-4 = 0.0005).

Picture a hiker in fog trying to reach the valley floor: feel the slope under your feet, step downhill, repeat. That is gradient descent. AdamW is a smarter hiker that adapts its step per weight.

Where in the codeIn train_tagger.py, these lines are the whole story: loss = F.cross_entropy(...), opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(), opt.step(). Everything else is plumbing.
Check yourselfWhat does "the model has 1.56M parameters" mean? What is the loss? What does the learning rate control? (Answers: 1.56 million adjustable numbers · a score of how wrong the guess is · how big each adjustment step is.)

Lesson 3 · NLP

Turning text into numbers

A model can only do arithmetic. So text goes through two conversions.

 "Rahul Sharma"  ──tokenizer──►  [3052, 811, 4020]  ──embedding──►  3 lists of 128 numbers each
   (text)                          (token ids)                          (vectors the model can use)

Step 1: Tokenization

Cut text into pieces (tokens) and give each piece an id number. Three options:

Cut byProblem
Whole wordsA new name or ID the model never saw becomes "unknown". The vocabulary also becomes huge.
Single charactersNothing is unknown, but sentences become very long and each letter means little.
Pieces (BPE)Common words are one piece, rare strings split into smaller known pieces. Nothing is unknown. This is what we built.

Worked example: how BPE learns its pieces

Tiny training text: low ×5, lower ×2, newest ×6. Start with every word as separate letters, then repeatedly merge the most frequent neighbouring pair.

RoundMost frequent pairCountNew tokenWords now
Start–––l o w · l o w e r · n e w e s t
1w + e8 (2 in lower, 6 in newest)wel o w · l o we r · n e we s t
2l + o7 (5 + 2)lolo w · lo we r · n e we s t
…repeat until the vocabulary has 8,000 tokens

Our bpe.py does exactly this. It keeps the list of merges in order; to tokenize new text it applies the earliest-learned merges first. A name it has never seen simply ends up as smaller pieces. Special rule from pretokenize.py: @ . - stay inside a word, so rahul@example.com is not shredded.

Key detail: each token also remembers its character position. "Sharma" = characters 6–12. That is how a label on a token turns back into a black bar over exact characters.

Step 2: Embeddings

A token id is just an index. The model needs meaning, so each id is looked up in a table (nn.Embedding, 8,000 rows × 128 columns) and becomes a vector of 128 numbers. At first the rows are random. Training moves them so that tokens that behave alike (two first names) end up with similar numbers. That table is 1,024,000 of our 1.56M parameters, two thirds of the model.

Check yourselfWhy BPE rather than whole words? Why does each token store character offsets? What is an embedding? (Answers: never unknown, small vocabulary · so predictions map back to exact characters · a learned list of 128 numbers for each token.)

Lesson 4 · NLP

Sequence labelling and BIO tags

Our model does not rewrite the sentence. It puts a sticker on every token. The sticker set is the BIO scheme:

TagMeaning
OOutside: not private
B-PERSONBeginning of a person's name
I-PERSONInside (continuing) the same name
 Send  the  report  to   Rahul     Sharma    at   rahul@example.com   .
  O     O     O      O   B-PERSON  I-PERSON  O    B-EMAIL             O

Why B and I? Without them, "Rahul Sharma met Amit Shah" would read as one four-word name. B marks where a new name starts.

We have 11 entity types, each with a B and an I, plus O: 2×11 + 1 = 23 tags (schema.py). The model's last layer outputs 23 scores for every token; the highest wins.

Spans: back to characters

A span = (start, end, label) in characters. Our data stores only spans (the single source of truth). Two functions in align.py convert in both directions:

  • to_bio: spans + token positions → tags, used to build training answers.
  • to_spans: tags + token positions → spans, used to turn predictions into redactions.
Check yourselfLabel by hand: "Priya works at Infosys." (Answer: B-PERSON O O B-ORGANIZATION O. The full stop is its own token.) Why 23 tags? (11 types × 2 + O.)

Lesson 5 · ATML

Attention: the one idea that matters

To label a word you often need the words around it. Attention lets each word look at every other word and borrow what is useful.

The problem

Is "Sharma" a name? On its own, unclear. But next to "Rahul", after "Send the report to", it clearly is. The model needs context. Older models read left to right and forgot. Attention reads everything at once.

The library analogy (Query, Key, Value)

Every word makes three versions of itself, using three small weight tables the model learns:

NameThink of it as
Query"What am I looking for?" (written on a sticky note)
Key"What do I contain?" (the label on each book's spine)
Value"The actual content I can give you" (the book itself)

Each word compares its query with every word's key. A good match = high score = "pay attention to this word". Then it collects a weighted mix of the values.

A worked example with real numbers

Suppose, when processing "Sharma", the match scores against the three words Rahul, report, to come out as 2.0, 1.0 and 0.1.

  1. Softmax turns scores into percentages that add to 100%: e2.0=7.39, e1.0=2.72, e0.1=1.11, total 11.21. Weights: 66%, 24%, 10%.
  2. New "Sharma" vector = 0.66 × value(Rahul) + 0.24 × value(report) + 0.10 × value(to).
  3. So "Sharma" now carries a lot of "I am next to Rahul" information, which makes B-PERSON / I-PERSON an easy call.

The real formula, in our code

scores = q @ k.transpose(-2, -1) / math.sqrt(self.dk)     # how much each word cares about each other word
scores = scores.masked_fill(~mask, very_negative)         # padding gets ~0 weight after softmax
w = scores.softmax(-1)                                    # percentages
out = w @ v                                               # weighted mix of values

Written maths: Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax(Q·Kᵀ / √dk) · V. The division by √dk only stops scores from growing too large (which would make softmax too extreme to learn from).

Multi-head attention

Do this 4 times in parallel, each "head" with its own Q/K/V tables, each free to watch for a different clue (the word before, a title like "Mr.", an @ sign). Then glue the 4 results together. With 128 numbers and 4 heads, each head works with 32. Our ablation: 4 heads beat 2 and 8.

Position: attention can't tell order by itself

Attention treats the sentence like a bag of words. We add a position signal (sine and cosine waves at different speeds) to each vector, so the model can tell "Rahul Sharma" from "Sharma Rahul". Fixed waves generalised better than a learned position table in our ablation.

Masks

Padding mask: sentences in a batch are padded to equal length; masking stops attention looking at padding. Causal mask: used only in the decoder, forbids looking at later words (lesson 9).

Check yourselfExplain Q, K, V with the library analogy. What does softmax do? Why do we need positional encoding? (Answers: Q = what I seek, K = what each word offers, V = the content · turns scores into percentages · attention alone ignores order.)

Lesson 6 · ATML

Building the Transformer from the pieces

One encoder block

 input x
   ├─► multi-head attention ──┐
   └──────────────────────────┴─► add (residual) ─► LayerNorm ─┐
                                                               ├─► feed-forward ─┐
                                                               └─────────────────┴─► add ─► LayerNorm ─► output
PartWhy it is there
AttentionMix in information from other words.
Residual ("add")Keep the original information, output = input + change. Makes deep stacks trainable because the signal has a short path through.
LayerNormRescales the numbers so they stay in a healthy range and training is stable.
Feed-forwardA small two-layer network applied to each word on its own (128→256→128) to "think about" what attention gathered.

Why stack several blocks?

Layer 1 might learn "this word follows 'to'". Layer 2 can combine that with other findings. Depth = more reasoning steps. Ablation proof: 2 → 4 layers gave +5.5 F1.

The whole tagger, with shapes

B = sentences per batch, T = tokens per sentence.

StepShape outCode
token idsB × Tids
embedding (× √128)B × T × 128self.emb(ids)
+ positions, dropoutB × T × 128self.drop(self.pos(...))
4 encoder blocksB × T × 128for blk in self.blocks: x = blk(x, mask)
linear headB × T × 23self.head(x)

Output: 23 scores per token. Pick the biggest, and you have the BIO tag. That is the entire main model: 61 lines in encoder_tagger.py on top of 127 lines in layers.py.

Dropout (0.1) randomly switches off 10% of numbers during training so the model can't lean on any single path. It helps against overfitting.

Lesson 7 · ATML

Training: how the model actually learns

The vocabulary of a training run

WordMeaning, with our numbers
Batch32 sentences processed together (faster, steadier).
EpochOne full pass over all ~40k training rows. We ran 25.
StepOne batch = one weight update.
Learning rateStep size, 5e-4. Too big: jumps over the valley. Too small: takes forever.
Warm-up + cosine decayStart gently for the first 5% of steps, peak, then shrink smoothly toward zero as the model settles.
Gradient clippingCap huge updates at 1.0 so one odd batch can't wreck the model.
Weight decayA small pull towards smaller weights; reduces overfitting.

Three datasets, three jobs

 TRAIN (39,806 rows)  the model learns from these
 VAL   (3,956 rows)   after each epoch we score here and keep the best checkpoint
 TEST  (3,993 rows)   final exam, never used for decisions
 + UNSEEN TEMPLATES (595) and CAPS (1,997): harder exams

Why not just test on training data? Because a model can memorise. Like a student who memorised last year's paper: perfect on it, lost on new questions. Testing on data the model never saw shows real ability.

Overfitting, in our own run

Training loss kept falling but validation F1 flattened at ~0.87 from epoch 20: the model was starting to memorise. We guard with dropout, weight decay, and by keeping only the best-validation checkpoint.

The overfit sanity check (clever, remember it)

Before the real run, train on only 10 sentences for 150 epochs with no dropout. A correctly wired model must memorise them, loss near 0. If not, there's a bug. This catches mistakes in minutes, not after hours of Colab.

A real bug story (great viva material)

The first full run scored F1 0.21. Cause: PyTorch's default embedding start values were ~11× larger than the position signal, so word identity drowned out position and attention saturated. Fix: initialise with small values (std d-0.5). Lesson: initialisation matters, and tests plus sanity checks find such bugs.

Why Colab?

Your laptop CPU can run the code but training is slow. A Colab GPU does thousands of multiplications in parallel. We wrote and tested on CPU (tiny runs), then trained on GPU. Data is rebuilt there by scripts, because the dataset licence forbids redistribution.

Check yourselfWhy three splits? What is an epoch? Why try to overfit 10 examples on purpose? (Answers: learn, choose, final exam · one pass over training data · proves the code can learn at all.)

Lesson 8 · NLP

Evaluation: measuring honestly

Why plain accuracy is useless here

Most tokens are O (not private). A model that outputs "O" everywhere is ~95% accurate and hides nothing. So we score spans, not tokens.

Precision, recall, F1 with a worked example

The text really contains 10 private spans. The model hides 9 spans, of which 8 are exactly right.

MetricQuestion it answersCalculation
PrecisionOf what we hid, how much was really private?8 / 9 = 0.889
RecallOf what was private, how much did we hide?8 / 10 = 0.800
F1One number balancing both (harmonic mean)2·P·R / (P+R) = 0.842

A span only counts as correct if start, end and label all match exactly (metrics.py). Hiding half a name counts as wrong.

For privacy, recall is the one that matters

  • Low precision = we hid harmless words too (annoying: "Mail me" became [PERSON_1] me).
  • Low recall = a private value stayed visible (the failure the product exists to prevent).

So we added three privacy-specific metrics:

MetricMeaning
Leaked spans% of private spans not fully covered. Ignores the label: hiding an email as [PHONE] still hides it.
Leaked characters% of private characters left visible (catches half-hidden names).
Over-redaction% of harmless characters wrongly hidden (usability).

Reading the headline numbers

Regex: precision 0.72 but recall 0.23: when it fires it is usually right, but it misses most things. The production hybrid: F1 0.933, 2.8% leaked.

Check yourselfA model hides 20 spans, 15 correct, and the text had 30 private spans. Compute P, R, F1. (P = 0.75, R = 0.50, F1 = 0.60.) Which is worse for privacy, low P or low R? (Low R.)

Lesson 9 · ATML

The encoder-decoder, and why it lost

Two kinds of tasks

TaskToolExample
Label each input wordEncoder onlyour tagger
Write new textEncoder + decodertranslation, summaries, our seq2seq experiment

How the decoder works

The encoder reads the whole input into rich vectors. The decoder then writes the output one token at a time, each new token chosen using (1) what it has written so far and (2) the encoder's reading of the input.

 input:  Send the report to Rahul Sharma at rahul@example.com.
 output: Send the report to [PERSON] at [EMAIL].     (written token by token)

A decoder block has three parts instead of two:

  1. Masked self-attention: look at the words written so far, never the future (causal mask: a triangle of allowed positions).
  2. Cross-attention: queries come from the decoder, keys and values from the encoder. This is "look back at the input".
  3. Feed-forward, each step with residual + LayerNorm.

Same MultiHeadAttention class in the code; passing kv=encoder_output turns it into cross-attention.

Teacher forcing and greedy decoding

  • Teacher forcing (training): at every step feed the decoder the correct previous word, not its own guess. Fast, stable learning.
  • Greedy decoding (using it): at every step pick the single most likely next token. If it errs once, every later token builds on the mistake.

Why it lost (the project's key finding)

It must reproduce every unchanged word perfectly and swap only private ones. Small models don't. On the test set only 23% of outputs were exactly right, ordinary words changed in 35% of rows, and it duplicated, corrupted or invented tokens. A tagger only labels, so it cannot damage the text. Right tool for the job.

Check yourselfWhy can't the decoder see future words during training? What is cross-attention? Why is tagging safer than generating? (Answers: it would cheat by copying the answer · decoder looking at the encoder's output · labels cannot alter text.)

Lesson 10 · ATML

Pretrained models, DistilBERT, ONNX and ablations

Pretraining and fine-tuning

Our model started knowing nothing, and had to learn English from ~40k sentences. DistilBERT was first trained by others on enormous amounts of text, so it already understands language; we only fine-tuned it (4 epochs) to our labels. Like hiring a graduate versus teaching a child to read.

Ours (from scratch)DistilBERT (fine-tuned)
Parameters1.56M65.2M (40× more)
Size5.5 MB (ONNX)~261 MB
F1 on test0.921 (hybrid 0.933)0.956
Used in product?YesNo, reference only

Honest summary: it is better; ours is far smaller and fully ours, and the hybrid closes much of the gap.

ONNX

A universal model file. After training, export_onnx.py converts tagger.pt (PyTorch only) into tagger.onnx, runnable without PyTorch, even in a browser, and verifies the outputs match (difference ~1e-5).

Ablation = controlled experiment

Change one thing, keep everything else equal, compare. Our results:

  • Layers 2 → 4: big gain. 4 → 6: small gain. Diminishing returns, so we picked 4.
  • Heads 2 / 4 / 8: 4 best.
  • Sinusoidal vs learned positions: similar on test, learned much worse on unseen templates.
  • Vocabulary 4k / 8k / 16k: 16k worse because rare tokens get too little training.

Caveat to say yourself: one random seed each, so trust the trends, not the decimals.

Hybrid engine

Combine rules and learning. Regex is perfect on rigid formats (PAN, URL). The model understands meaning (names, places). Where they overlap, a priority rule decides (precise regex > model > loose regex). Our first rule scored worse than the model alone; we measured, found loose regex was overriding correct model labels, and fixed it. That is science working properly.
